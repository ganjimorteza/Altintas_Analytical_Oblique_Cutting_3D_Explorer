How to use the models

Adjust inclination i and normal rake αn independently in either tab. Change βa, τs, b and h using the shared panel above. Drag a model to rotate it. In the first tab, use the mouse wheel to zoom and the view selector to restore a preset. Settings are retained when switching tabs; the two tabs have independent controls.

In the first tab, Top (workpiece Xw–Yw plane) makes the cutting-edge inclination easy to inspect. The rectangular stock, its frame (Xw,Yw,Zw) and the cutting direction V are stationary. The frame (x,y,z) is defined exactly as in Altintas Fig. 2.5: y lies along the cutting edge, x lies on the cut surface perpendicular to that edge, and z is the surface normal. The tool-local frame turns by −i relative to the workpiece axes. The Normal to tool plane Pn view shows rake-face changes with αn. You can independently toggle the two coordinate frames.

The velocity arrows follow V + Vs = Vc. In the velocity triangle, Vs (O) is a translated copy of the shear-velocity vector, drawn from the origin.

Model notes, notation and reference

Reference frames: Workpiece edges define stationary world axes Xw, Yw, Zw. The book’s local axes x, y, z turn with the tool: y is tangent to the cutting edge, x is normal to the edge but tangent to the machined surface, and z is perpendicular to that surface. The velocity V remains along Xw as the tool rotates. These two frames coincide only when i = 0°. World-to-local conversion is explicitly applied to the first three-dimensional view. The second tab plots force vectors in the tool-local frame.

This educational visualization uses an ideal sharp, straight cutting edge and a single shear plane. The chip-flow and shear angles use an algebraic quartic reduction of the five-angle maximum-shear-stress closure (Eqs. 2.48–2.52). The original unsquared scalar relation and the original five equations are independently evaluated to select and verify the admissible root. The average friction angle βa is adjustable. Tool and workpiece dimensions are schematic. Edge-radius forces and chip deformation are outside the model.

The force sphere is a geometric extension of the Merchant circle: orthogonal force pairs form right triangles inscribed in a sphere whose diameter is the resultant vector. In the second tab, C = Fc/2 is the center position and the radius is |Fc|/2. The actual shear-force contribution is calculated from τs bh and Eq. (2.53); force-sphere drawing scales to its current resultant force magnitude. The first tab uses normalized force-vector directions and velocity ratios.

Force directions: the first tab shows forces on the chip, with resultant R; the second shows forces on the tool, with resultant Fc. Corresponding vectors therefore point in opposite directions.

Numerical methods: The primary angle solution is obtained by bracketing real roots of the quartic polynomial in tan η and rejecting roots that fail the original unsquared equation. A separate bisection solver directly evaluates the original velocity Eq. (2.50), with Eqs. (2.48), (2.49), (2.51), and (2.52) used to recover the remaining angles. Force components are cross-checked against Eqs. (2.53) and (2.59). This is a comparison with a numerical solution of the book’s equations, not a reimplementation of its relaxation scheme.

Reference: Yusuf Altintas, Manufacturing Automation: Metal Cutting Mechanics, Machine Tool Vibrations, and CNC Design, 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2012, Chapter 2.

Open this HTML file directly in a current desktop browser such as Microsoft Edge or Google Chrome. All code and graphics are included; no internet connection or installation is required. This is an algebraic consistency demonstration, not experimental validation and not a claim of improved physical accuracy. The admissible shear branch requires 0° < φn < 90° and Fc > 0; otherwise the force values are marked N/A. It does not include edge-radius, ploughing, flank, chip curl, temperature, or nonuniform shear-zone contributions.
